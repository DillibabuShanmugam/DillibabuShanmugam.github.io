Security for Physical AI Hardware

Physical AI puts neural networks into devices that sit in the open: cameras, sensors, controllers and other edge hardware. Anyone who can touch such a device can attack the chip that runs the model. My work on Physical AI security asks what happens to AI hardware under physical fault attacks such as electromagnetic pulses and voltage or clock glitches.

AI hardware often stores numbers in the OCP Microscaling (MX) formats (MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4, MXINT8), where a block of values shares one scale. That sharing saves memory and energy, but it also concentrates risk. One fault in the shared scale corrupts every value in the block. I compared the MX formats on a RISC-V SoC with an MX coprocessor whose internal state can be read out through a scan chain. MXFP8-E4M3 was the most vulnerable format both in gate-level simulation and in electromagnetic fault injection on the CAPRI1 test chip. Details are on the MX format fault analysis page.

On an FPGA, 2,040 undervoltage and clock-glitch tests showed which wrong outputs came from the processor and which came from a corrupted shared exponent. From these results I proposed two log formats, LOG8-SUM and LOG8-MAX, and recommended redundant processors and exponent protection against silent data corruption. Both MX papers (ACM GLSVLSI 2026 and ACNS-AIHWS 2026) were Best Paper nominees.

To sort large amounts of fault data, I use μScan, which applies graph neural networks and a fine-tuned language model to scan-chain snapshots to tell normal processor states from faulty ones. I am now studying fault behavior on an open-source RISC-V GPU, where one fault can reach every thread at once. The hardware that runs these models is described on the edge AI inference on RISC-V page.
