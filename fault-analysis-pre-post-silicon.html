Fault Analysis Using Pre- and Post-Silicon Models

A fault attack makes a chip compute the wrong result by disturbing it with an electromagnetic pulse, a laser, or a voltage or clock glitch. To defend against it, a designer needs to know where faults start and how they spread, ideally before the chip is built. My work builds pre-silicon fault models and checks them on post-silicon test chips that can read out their own internal state.

On the CAPRI1 RISC-V test chip, I modeled current loops and supply voltage drop from the finished layout to predict where electromagnetic faults would start. I then fired 331 electromagnetic pulses at the chip and read its state through the scan chain. 29 of 41 first bit flips fell in the 20% of the chip that the model flagged as most at risk (European Test Symposium 2026). One bit flip in the register file was followed through a memory overwrite to a skipped PIN check.

That trace from layout to software is the idea behind FaultDetective (IACR TCHES 2024), which explains a fault across the whole stack. CAPRI6 (IEEE MDTS 2025), a six-core lockstep SoC with a full scan chain, supports cycle-accurate root-cause analysis. Because some faults change the processor state without changing its output, μScan (AIHWS 2025) classifies scan-chain snapshots from simulation and from CAPRI6, with 92% average accuracy over six test cases, and links two weaknesses to CWE-835, CWE-1332 and CWE-691. GlitchGlück (USENIX WOOT 2025) uses state transition graphs to guide fault injection toward software vulnerabilities.

The same pre- and post-silicon method drives the MX format fault analysis, where gate-level simulation and EMFI on CAPRI1 agreed on the most vulnerable format. The side-channel counterpart is on the cross-layer side-channel analysis page.
